The Prescription Trap

A simple test could solve the overprescription crisis

Genetic analysis could help GPs tailor medicines to stop older patients taking dangerous mixtures

Shaun Lintern, Health Editor
Saturday October 03 2026, 8.15pm BST, The Sunday Times
Listen
0:00 / • 5:33 min

A simple blood test could tell GPs which medicines are the safest and most effective for elderly patients taking multiple prescriptions, according to researchers behind a pioneering new trial.

The five-year, £3m study, run by scientists at the University of Southampton and the National Institute for Health and Care Research, will examine the feasibility of GPs using genetic testing as part of regular medication reviews for their elderly patients to help solve Britain’s overprescribing epidemic.

More than 90 per cent of people have at least one genetic variation that can mean they may respond poorly to certain drugs when given the standard prescription amount. Some patients may not respond to the drug they need, putting them at risk. For others the drug’s impact could be stronger than intended, making them ill or leaving them with side effects like dizziness or falls.

The overprescribing of drugs, particularly to older people, is thought to cost the NHS more than £2bn a year, and two thirds of hospital admissions in over-65s linked to preventable medicine-related side effects.

The researchers hope the study could herald the beginning of truly personalised medicine regimes for frail older people and drastically cut the thousands of people admitted to hospital.

The Sunday Times has launched a campaign to stop the prescription trap that is harming elderly patients. Our three calls for action are:

  • All medical students must learn the risks of mixing medicines and receive training for how and when to deprescribe
  • The NHS should ensure that anyone over 65 who is considered frail and is taking ten or more medications is invited for an annual review of their drug regimen
  • 1,850 more geriatricians must be trained by 2030 to ensure there is one doctor for every 500 patients aged 85 and over

Dr Kinda Ibrahim, associate professor at the University of Southampton, said the trial was designed to help tackle the so-called polypharmacy crisis. She said: “We have millions of people already taking high volumes of medications. We don’t know if the medication works for them, if they are causing any harm or side-effects, so we wanted to test if doing a genetic test after being on these medications would be useful information to the GPs and pharmacists to make effective prescribing decisions.”

The team behind the study is designing a test that will use a blood sample to test for genes known to affect medicines commonly prescribed to older people such as antidepressants, opioids and pain medications as well as statins, blood pressure drugs and proton pump inhibitor medicines such as omeprazole, often prescribed for chronic acid reflux and stomach ulcers. More than 1,000 patients aged 65 and over who are taking five or more regular medicines will be enrolled on the trial. Half will receive a standard medication review and the other half will have a review informed by their genetic test results. It will run across 30 GP practices in Birmingham and the southwest of England.

Advertisement

Ibrahim, 44, who trained as a pharmacist, said the impact could lead to a reduction in hospital admissions and improve outcomes for patients.

“You are more likely to improve people’s treatment responses. This is the assumption that we are making, but we can’t definitely say until we do the trial and see whether there’s a difference to what we are already doing,” she said.

Older patients are supposed to have regular medication reviews but many GPs are not carrying them out. Only 16 per cent of frail patients, who are often living with mobility issues, had a review in 2024-25.

The government is working on a new plan for improving care for frail older people which is expected to be published early next year. It will set standards all areas of the country will be expected to follow.

Penny Dash, chair of NHS England, said every frail patient in England should have a care plan drawn up by their GP to manage their medications and help keep them out of hospital. She said “small, fragmented GP practices, working in isolation” were not capable of delivering what was needed, adding: “We need to do a much better job at planning care. Everybody should have a care plan. We should have single patient records for everybody.

Advertisement

“If they’re older and frail or have a chronic disease or long-term condition, we should know what is the plan for them,” she said. “We can’t do all of this in small, subscale units. We need to bring those GP practices together. We’ve got to change the models of care. We can’t just keep talking about it and not do it.”

Dash suggested artificial intelligence could play a central role in identifying elderly patients on dangerous combinations of drugs: “We’ve got great technology that allows people to look at what are the different combinations of drugs that people are on and how can we rationalise those. That will have to be a key part of the frailty plan and how we do a better job of prescribing.”

The Royal College of GPs (RCGP) rejected the suggestion that smaller practices were incapable of delivering good frailty care. Professor Victoria Tzortziou Brown, chair of the RCGP council, said: “The evidence does not support an assumption that bigger practices automatically provide better care. Indeed, studies have found that smaller practices can perform better on important measures of patient experience, including continuity of care and overall satisfaction.”

She said GPs were already under significant pressure, with the share of the NHS budget received by general practice at its lowest in a decade, and that any new frailty strategy “must be properly resourced”.
